What Tesla filed to buy from EMAG — and what a ‘cartel office’ is

The Elon Times · Friday, November 27, 2026 · A ledger story

On November 23 Tesla notified Germany’s Federal Cartel Office of an asset purchase from EMAG, a Swabian machine-tool maker. The notice became public November 27 as case B5-132/26. The machines do precision, electrochemical and laser work on metal. The filing does not say where Tesla would use them, and the review is still pending.

On November 23, 2026, Tesla notified Germany’s Federal Cartel Office — the Bundeskartellamt — that it wants to buy assets from the EMAG Group, a machine-tool maker based in Salach, near Stuttgart. The office published the filing on November 27 as case B5-132/26. The notice covers manufacturing systems and machines for the precise production and electrochemical machining of metal parts, and for laser processing. No purchase price was disclosed, and the review is still pending. (Bundeskartellamt notice via finanznachrichten.de, Nov. 27, 2026)

The case is listed as a Vermögenserwerb, an asset acquisition. In other words, Tesla is buying specific machines and manufacturing systems from EMAG, not the company. The notice does not list exactly which machines or sites are included. Automotive World, citing Reuters, reported that Tesla declined to comment and that EMAG did not respond to a request for comment. (Automotive World, Nov. 27, 2026; Handelsblatt, Nov. 27, 2026)

Some readers assume the cartel office only looks at company takeovers. It also reviews purchases of significant assets, which is what this is. A filing is not a closed deal: until the office clears it, or the first-phase clock runs out without a deeper review, the machines have not changed hands.

What the headlines said, and what the filing says

Much of the coverage turned this into a takeover story: Tesla “buying,” “taking over,” even Musk personally “reaching for” a German company. The filing says none of that. It uses one word, Vermögenserwerb, an asset acquisition: machines and manufacturing systems, not the company. Headlines have a few words to work with, and “takeover” sells better than “asset purchase.” Read the filing first and the headline second. When the two disagree, the record wins.

A machine-tool maker in Salach

EMAG’s roots go back to 1867, when it was founded in Bautzen, in Saxony, as an iron foundry and engineering works. It was re-established in 1952 in Eislingen, in Baden-Württemberg, building lathes and special-purpose machines, and moved in 1969 to Salach, still its group headquarters, between Stuttgart and Ulm. (EMAG, company history)

The company makes machine tools and complete production lines for metal parts in volume: vertical turning, milling, drilling, grinding, and gear and shaft machining. In 1992 it introduced the VSC, which it calls the world’s first vertical turning center with a pick-up spindle — a machine whose spindle picks up a workpiece, holds it while tools cut it from below, and sets the finished part down. The same family of machines is used on round parts such as gears, shafts, bearing rings and rotors. EMAG also sells systems for electrochemical machining and for laser welding and processing, and it markets manufacturing cells for e-mobility parts, including rotor shafts, stator housings and planetary gears. (EMAG, vertical turning; EMAG, automotive and mobility)

The company has been under strain. Business Insider, whose figures were widely repeated, reported that EMAG lost €42 million on €496 million of revenue in 2024, and that about a year earlier it had announced 455 job cuts in Germany, roughly 300 of them in Salach. Chronicle has not independently checked those figures against EMAG’s 2024 annual report. SWR, citing that report, put the 2024 loss at about €43 million. (Electrek, Nov. 25, 2026, citing Business Insider; SWR, Nov. 27, 2026)

Tesla had already set up a company at EMAG’s door. Germany’s company register lists Tesla Machining Technologies Management SE under HRB 315756 at the Munich district court, with €120,000 in share capital and a business address of Austraße 24 in Salach, EMAG’s headquarters. Electrek, reading the register, reported that the company was first entered on August 26 as “Blitz 26-299 SE,” a pre-registered shell, and that Tesla renamed it in November. Business Insider Germany, cited by Electrek and Handelsblatt, said its managers include Maximilian Stromberger, Tesla Germany’s head of sales, an American robotics engineer, and the head of human resources at Tesla’s Grünheide plant. Neither Tesla nor EMAG confirmed those details. (Electrek, Nov. 25, 2026)

Local and national press have also reported that about 220 EMAG employees would move to Tesla, and that the two companies have already been working together on humanoid robots. Those figures and that purpose come from the Stuttgarter Nachrichten and Business Insider. Tesla and EMAG have not confirmed them. (Electrek, Nov. 25, 2026; TeslaNorth, Nov. 27, 2026)

What these machines are used for

The easiest way to picture a vertical turning machine is as a very precise, very repeatable way to shape a round metal part. The spindle picks the part up, the tools stay fixed below it, and chips fall away. EMAG’s own applications list includes gears, shafts, brake discs, and the rotor shafts and stator housings of electric motors. That is ordinary high-volume metal cutting: a hard tool removing metal by contact. (EMAG, vertical turning)

Electrochemical machining is different. There is no blade in the usual sense. The metal part is made the positive electrode, or anode. A shaped tool is the negative electrode, or cathode. A conductive liquid, the electrolyte, flows through a narrow gap between them. When current is applied, metal ions dissolve off the workpiece and are carried away by the liquid. The tool does not touch the part. Because the metal is dissolved rather than cut, the process does not raise burrs — the sharp leftover edges that conventional machining leaves behind and that later have to be ground or filed off. The hardness of the alloy does not control how fast the metal comes off, so the method is used on tough materials that eat ordinary cutting tools, and it can form small, precise features. Precision electrochemical machining, or PECM, is EMAG’s tighter version of the same idea: a smaller gap, a pulsed current, and a more exact copy of the tool’s shape. EMAG describes PECM for parts such as turbine blisks and turbocharger components, and notes that ECM and PECM do not form burrs. (Open University, Electrochemical Machining; EMAG, PECM)

Laser processing, in EMAG’s catalog, is largely about joining. EMAG LaserTec, the name given in 2019 to the group’s former automation unit in Heubach, builds laser-welding and thermal-joining systems used to fasten drivetrain and clutch parts without the heavy threaded connections those assemblies once needed. The company has also used lasers for cleaning and for hard-coating work such as brake discs. The cartel-office notice says only “laser processing”; it does not name a machine family or a part. (EMAG, LaserTec rename, 2019)

EMAG also publishes a robotics section devoted to three parts used in the joints of humanoid robots: flexsplines, wave generators and planetary gears. The first two make up a strain-wave gear, a compact gearbox that turns a motor’s fast spin into a slow, strong joint movement with very little backlash. Planetary gears, EMAG says, are used in the larger joints that carry more load. The company says its machines can hold flexsplines to ISO 1328 Class 4 gear quality, with runout under 5 micrometers, and can repeat that in volume production. Those are EMAG’s claims about what its machines can do, not a statement about any Tesla order. (EMAG, precision components for humanoid robots)

The ‘cartel office’ is not a gang

The name is the part that sounds like a crime syndicate. It isn’t one.

The Bundeskartellamt is Germany’s Federal Cartel Office, the country’s independent competition authority. Its job is to protect competition. In German and European law, a Kartell is a cartel in the antitrust sense: companies secretly agreeing to fix prices, divide markets or otherwise stop competing. Prosecuting those agreements is one of the office’s tasks. Merger control is another. The office also polices the abuse of a dominant position. It is a government regulator, based in Bonn, not a gang and not a general business court. (Bundeskartellamt, tasks; Bundeskartellamt, merger control)

Deals above a certain size have to be notified before they can be carried out. A “concentration” under the law covers more than buying a company. Buying significant assets, such as a production site or a business division, can also qualify, which is why an asset purchase shows up in the same list as a merger. The usual thresholds are about size: the companies together must have had worldwide turnover of more than €500 million in the previous year, with at least one above €50 million in Germany and another above €17.5 million. There is also a transaction-value path, generally when the purchase price exceeds €400 million and the target is active in Germany even if its German turnover is smaller. Chronicle does not know which threshold brought this filing in, and the notice does not say. (Bundeskartellamt, obligation to notify; GWB § 35; GWB § 40)

The first-phase review lasts one month from the day the office receives a complete notification. If it needs a closer look, it can open a second phase, which extends the examination to five months in total. Until the office clears the deal, or the first-phase month ends without a deeper review, the concentration may not be put into effect. The Tesla/EMAG notice gives November 23 as the date of notification. If that filing was complete on that day, a first-phase decision would typically fall around late December. Chronicle has not seen a statement that the file was deemed complete on the 23rd, so that date is a rule-of-thumb, not a confirmed deadline.

The ‘cartel’ in the office’s name is a reminder of the law it enforces, not a description of Tesla or EMAG. A public listing means the office has a file. It does not mean the office has found a problem, and it does not mean the purchase is approved.

Why this might matter for Tesla

The filing itself does not say what Tesla would do with the equipment. That has to be stated plainly before any reading of the surrounding facts.

What the public record does show is a company trying to make more of its hardware in-house, and a robot program that has reached early production while still wrestling with precise mechanical parts.

On May 9, 2026, Tesla built the last Model S and the last Model X at Fremont, then decommissioned those lines and began installing first-generation Optimus lines in the same space. In its Q3 2026 Update, furnished October 21, Tesla said Optimus production had begun on the high-volume lines in Fremont, and that the bots coming off the line were its first production-quality units. On the earnings call that day, Elon Musk called it “early production,” said reaching about 20,000 units a week would take at least a year, maybe longer, and said customer deliveries were likely “towards the end of next year.” Tesla’s capacity table listed Fremont Optimus as Early Ramp and Texas Optimus as Construction. (Tesla, last S/X at Fremont, May 9, 2026; SEC, Q3 2026 Update, Oct. 21, 2026)

Tesla has designed its own actuators since the early Optimus prototypes. At AI Day in 2022 it showed a production-oriented prototype with Tesla-designed actuators; the Optimus Gen 2 video in December 2023 said the robot used Tesla-designed actuators and sensors. On the October 21, 2026 call, Konstantinos Laskaris said the hand was one of the most challenging hardware areas and that “some bespoke gears that are carrying a lot of burden for consumption and for reliability” were still being developed. Musk agreed that the remaining hardware difficulty was mostly the hand. (Tesla, AI Day 2022; Tesla, Optimus Gen 2, Dec. 2023; Tesla Q3 2026 earnings webcast; Motley Fool transcript, Oct. 22, 2026)

The same months have Tesla enlarging the factory side of its other volume bet, the Cybercab. Production of the two-seat robotaxi began at Gigafactory Texas in the first half of 2026. On November 5, Musk said Tesla would wind down Cybertruck production there around mid-2027 to make room for more Cybercab output. On November 16, Tesla told Reuters it would invest €3 billion in an additional facility at Giga Berlin in Grünheide, aimed at up to one million Cybercabs a year, with a permit application due by the end of 2026, construction in the first half of 2027, and production in 2029. (Reuters via Street Insider, Nov. 16, 2026)

Separately, Tesla and SpaceX have announced Terafab, an advanced chip plant, first described in Austin in March 2026 and later sited as a volume campus in Grimes County, Texas. That project is about logic and memory chips, not metal-cutting machines. It belongs in this picture only as another attempt to pull a scarce manufacturing step in-house.

Salach and Grünheide are in the same country, not next door. Salach sits east of Stuttgart; Giga Berlin is in Brandenburg, hundreds of kilometers to the northeast. The documented German connection in the press is the new machining company at EMAG’s address, and the reported presence of Grünheide’s HR chief among its managers, not a claim that the machines would be installed beside the Model Y line.

Tesla has not said these machines are for Optimus, or for Cybercab, or for any other named product. The filing does not say where the equipment would run. What stands out is that the advertised work of these machines — precise gears, shafts, hard alloys, burr-free features, laser-joined assemblies — sits next to a known hard point in Tesla’s robot program, the custom gears and actuators that still have to be made reliably and in volume, and next to a broader habit of bringing manufacturing steps inside the company. EMAG itself markets machines for flexsplines, wave generators and planetary gears in humanoid-robot actuators. A Tesla machining company already sits at EMAG’s headquarters. Press reports of a staff move and a robotics purpose fit that picture, and remain unconfirmed. The same machines could also cut e-motor parts for cars, including a Cybercab ramp. None of that is a stated Tesla purpose. Price, scope and destination are still unknown, and the office has not cleared the deal.
